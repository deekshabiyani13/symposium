The Logic Trials

Three questions stand between you and the truth

Trial I — The Philosopher's Paradox

"The unexamined life is not worth living." — Socrates died for this claim. What, then, is the examined life ultimately for?

✦   Precisely. Socrates did not examine life to arrive — he examined it to move. Much like a certain someone who never stops asking questions, even when the answers might inconvenience him. She finds this extraordinarily attractive.

Trial II — The Mythological Riddle

Which god presides over both the revelry of wine and the theater of tragedy — knowing that the highest joy always contains within it the seed of sorrow?

✦   Dionysus — who taught the Greeks that the most profound experiences contain their opposite. That a wine bar, for instance, might be the site of the most genuine philosophy. Stoa awaits its willing contradiction.

Trial III — The Logical Proposition

If all great evenings contain: good wine, sharper minds, and one specific person — and tonight offers all three — what does logic demand you do?

✦   Modus ponens is undefeated. The conclusion follows necessarily. Even Aristotle would approve of this particular deduction — though he would have written 400 pages about it first.
